# Hypothesis: Same-Day Intraday Momentum in NQ Futures — First 30 Minutes Predicting the Last 30 Minutes

Does the direction and magnitude of NQ price movement in the first 30 minutes of a session predict the direction of movement in the final 30 minutes of that same session? The canonical intraday momentum effect, documented in equity markets, proposes that early-session returns continue into late-session returns — a same-day continuation rather than a reversal. The hypothesis under test is that this effect exists in NQ futures at the 30-minute window scale and survives the friction of real execution: the spread paid on entry and exit, and the per-side commission charged on each trade.

Two competing accounts make different predictions. The first holds that intraday momentum is a genuine feature of market microstructure and order flow: early-session price movement reflects information or behavioral patterns that persist through the day, so a positive first half-hour return predicts a positive final half-hour return, and vice versa. The second account holds that any such effect, if it existed historically, has been arbitraged away in modern NQ futures, or that it is too small relative to transaction costs to be tradable. Under this account, the first half-hour return contains no predictive power for the last half-hour return after accounting for the cost of entry, exit, and the bid-ask spread.

An observation that discriminates these accounts would be a systematic tendency for NQ's final 30-minute return to align with the sign of its first 30-minute return, measured across many sessions, in a way that persists after paying the spread on both entry and exit and the commission on both sides of a round trip. The effect must be large enough that a strategy trading on this signal — entering on the first half-hour's close and exiting on the final half-hour's close — generates positive net returns despite these frictions. Conversely, if the final 30 minutes show no directional relationship to the first 30 minutes, or if the relationship is too weak to overcome the cost of trading it, the hypothesis is falsified.

This hypothesis does not address whether the effect is stronger or weaker in certain market regimes, volatility states, or time periods; it asks the simple version first. It does not propose a specific entry or exit rule beyond the session-relative timing windows. It does not claim the effect is stable across the full dataset or that it will persist into the future; it asks whether it exists in this data at this granularity. The papers document the effect in equities and discuss its mechanisms, but provide limited direct evidence on NQ futures specifically at this time scale; this campaign tests whether the effect replicates on NQ trades under realistic execution costs.
